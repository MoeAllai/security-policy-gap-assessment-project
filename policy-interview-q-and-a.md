# Policy Interview Q&A

## Purpose

This document prepares interview-ready answers based on the Security Policy Gap Assessment Project.

The goal is to explain the project clearly during interviews and demonstrate practical understanding of policy review, gap assessment, framework mapping, corrective action planning, and security governance.

---

## Q1: Can you walk me through this policy gap assessment project?

**Answer:**

This project simulates a Junior GRC Analyst assignment for a mock SaaS company improving its security governance program.

I reviewed a set of security policies, created a policy inventory, identified gaps, mapped those gaps to NIST CSF and ISO 27001-style concepts, created a corrective action plan, documented findings, and prepared an executive summary.

The main goal was to determine whether the company’s policies were complete, current, owned, approved, and strong enough to support audit readiness and customer security reviews.

---

## Q2: What policies did you review?

**Answer:**

I reviewed policies related to:

- Information security
- Access control
- Password and MFA
- Incident response
- Vendor risk management
- Data classification
- Acceptable use
- Business continuity
- Change management
- Evidence retention

These policies were selected because they are commonly reviewed during audits, customer security reviews, and internal compliance assessments.

---

## Q3: What were the biggest gaps you identified?

**Answer:**

The biggest gaps were:

- Access Control Policy did not require quarterly access reviews
- Privileged access requirements were incomplete
- MFA exception handling was unclear
- Incident response customer notification requirements were not defined
- Vendor risk policy did not fully define high-risk vendor review requirements
- Restricted data handling requirements were not detailed enough
- Evidence retention standards were not standardized

These gaps matter because they affect audit readiness, access governance, incident communication, vendor oversight, data protection, and evidence quality.

---

## Q4: Why are policy owners important?

**Answer:**

Policy owners are important because someone must be responsible for maintaining, reviewing, updating, and enforcing each policy.

Without a documented owner, policies may become outdated or inconsistently applied.

In this project, I recommended assigning a clear owner for every policy, such as Compliance Manager, IT Manager, IT Security, HR, Operations, or Engineering.

---

## Q5: Why do policies need annual review dates?

**Answer:**

Annual review dates help prove that policies are maintained and still aligned with business, security, and compliance needs.

During audits or customer security reviews, companies may be asked to show that policies are reviewed regularly.

If review dates are missing, it may look like the policies are outdated or not actively governed.

---

## Q6: How did you map gaps to NIST CSF?

**Answer:**

I mapped each gap to relevant NIST CSF functions and categories.

For example, access review gaps mapped to the Protect function because they relate to identity management and access control.

Incident notification gaps mapped to the Respond function because they relate to incident communication and response.

Vendor risk gaps mapped to Govern and Identify because they relate to supply chain risk management and third-party risk oversight.

---

## Q7: How did you map gaps to ISO 27001 concepts?

**Answer:**

I mapped the gaps to ISO 27001-style control concepts such as access control, privileged access management, supplier relationship security, incident management, information classification, business continuity, change management, and documented information.

The goal was not to claim formal ISO certification work, but to show practical understanding of how security policy gaps connect to recognized information security control areas.

---

## Q8: What is the difference between a gap assessment and an audit?

**Answer:**

A gap assessment identifies weaknesses compared to a target framework, policy expectation, or control requirement.

An audit formally tests whether controls are designed and operating effectively, usually using evidence and defined audit criteria.

In this project, I performed a policy gap assessment by reviewing policy content, identifying missing requirements, mapping those gaps to frameworks, and recommending corrective actions.

---

## Q9: Why is MFA exception management important?

**Answer:**

MFA exception management is important because exceptions can weaken authentication controls.

If exceptions are not approved, tracked, reviewed, and time-bound, they may create unnecessary security risk.

In this project, I recommended requiring an owner, business justification, approval, compensating control, review date, and expiration date for every MFA exception.

---

## Q10: Why is evidence retention part of a policy project?

**Answer:**

Evidence retention is important because audits and customer security reviews depend on reliable documentation.

Even if a company performs a control, it may still have trouble proving it if evidence is missing, inconsistently named, or stored in different places.

In this project, I recommended standardizing evidence folder structure, naming convention, ownership, retention periods, and acceptable evidence types.

---

## Q11: How would you explain the business impact of weak policies?

**Answer:**

Weak policies create unclear expectations.

If policies do not clearly define ownership, frequency, approval, evidence, or enforcement, teams may perform controls inconsistently.

That can lead to audit findings, poor evidence quality, unauthorized access, delayed incident communication, weak vendor review, or mishandling of sensitive data.

---

## Q12: How would you prioritize remediation?

**Answer:**

I would prioritize remediation based on risk level, audit impact, and business impact.

The first priorities would be access control, privileged access, MFA exceptions, incident response notification, vendor risk requirements, data classification handling, and evidence retention.

These areas are commonly reviewed during audits and can create higher risk if not clearly documented.

---

## Q13: What would you do after policies are updated?

**Answer:**

After policies are updated, I would validate that each policy includes ownership, review date, approval history, clear control requirements, exception handling, and evidence expectations.

I would also confirm that updated policies are approved, communicated to the right teams, and stored in the correct location.

Then I would collect evidence showing that the corrective actions were completed.
